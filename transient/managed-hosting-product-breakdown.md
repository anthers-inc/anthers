# Managed Hosting Product Breakdown   
Bluebell's infrastructure model funds platform development and shared services without ever taking a cut of creator payments.    
However, our [V1 design plan](managed-hosting-product-breakdown-v1.md) revealed a presentation problem: even though every creator comes out ahead compared to YouTube, the tier-based pricing structure ($69/mo for Canopy, $1,200+/mo for Redwood, etc.) looks prohibitively expensive on spec. Creators who are used to YouTube's $0 hosting (subsidized by ads and a 45% revenue share) would balk at the sticker price before ever seeing the revenue column.   
This new plan reframes the entire model around a simple structural insight: **bandwidth is consumed by viewers, not creators.** A creator uploading a video costs almost nothing. The infrastructure costs are generated by people *watching*. So the invoice should be on the viewer side — deducted transparently from pool earnings — not presented as a creator-facing bill.   
The math is identical. The psychology is completely different.   
   
# How It Works: The User Side   
 --- 
A user's monthly subscription ($10/mo at launch) flows through three layers in order:   
## Layer 1: Community Resilience Fund (3%)   
A small, fixed portion of every subscription is allocated to the Community Resilience Fund before anything else. On a $10/mo subscription, this is just $0.30.   
The CRF serves two purposes:   
- **Lifting small creators.** New creators have real infrastructure costs (storage, compute) but minimal viewership. The CRF covers the gap, ensuring no creator pays out of pocket more than they earn. This replicates what YouTube offers (free hosting subsidized by ads) through community funding rather than advertising.   
- **Viral surge protection.** When a creator's traffic spikes dramatically beyond their rolling average — a video gets shared widely, a collaboration brings a flood of new viewers — the CRF absorbs the excess infrastructure cost. A viral moment should be the best day of a creator's career, not the day they get an unexpected bandwidth bill.   
   
The pitch to subscribers is straightforward:    
```
"With less than $1/month, your subscription helps new creators of all kinds get started and protects creators when their content takes off." 
```
This is transparent, it's small, and it directly strengthens the ecosystem.   
We can think about the fund sizing as follows:   
- At 50,000 subscribers ($500K/mo total pool), 3% allocation = $15,000/mo   
- Budget:    
    - ~$5,000 for small creator subsidies (500 creators × ~$10 avg)   
    - ~$2,500–5,000 for surge protection (5–10 events/mo × ~$500 avg)   
    - Remainder accumulates as reserve, or for special projects like contests/RFCs   
   
Once the reserve reaches a defined threshold (e.g., 6 months of average payouts), the allocation percentage decreases, returning more money to the creator pool.   
   
## Layer 2: Infrastructure Baseline (variable, typically 0.2–3%)   
After the CRF deduction, each view-minute the subscriber generates carries a tiny infrastructure cost that is deducted from the pool allocation *before* it reaches the creator. This is the cost of delivering the content the subscriber actually watched.   
This is not a fee the subscriber sees as a separate line item. It's built into the pool math: when the system calculates "this subscriber watched 40 minutes of Creator X this month," it computes both the creator's earnings and the infrastructure cost for those 40 minutes, and the creator receives the net amount.   
**The critical constraint:** a subscriber's infrastructure baseline can never exceed their subscription amount. If a user watches so much content that infrastructure costs would theoretically exceed their $10/mo., the costs are capped and the CRF absorbs the overage. In practice, this is extremely unlikely—infrastructure costs per view-minute are roughly 250–300× smaller than the pool payout rate—but the cap exists as a guarantee that subscribing to Bluebell never produces a surprise bill.   
The infrastructure cost per view-minute is roughly $0.00002–0.00004 at volume, compared to the pool payout rate of approx. $0.0065/view-minute. For a subscriber watching 25 hours/month, total infrastructure cost is roughly $0.03–0.06/month — less than 1% of their subscription. The rest flows to creators.   
   
## Layer 3: Creator Allocation (the remainder)   
Everything after the CRF and infrastructure baseline flows to creators through two mechanisms:   
### **Baseline allocation (automatic)**   
By default, a user's remaining subscription is distributed proportionally based on consumption time — how much of each creator's content they actually watched. This requires no user action and produces a fair outcome for passive users, which will be the majority. A stepping function enforces minimum thresholds: if you consume a meaningful proportion of your time from one creator, they receive at least X% of your subscription. This protects creators from having their baseline yanked away and prevents gaming.   
### **Discretionary allocation (manual)**   
On top of the baseline, users have the ability to adjust the proportions of how their non-baseline money is distributed per-channel. This allows them to express value rather than just attention — boosting a small creator they love over a background music channel they leave on in the background. Critically, the discretionary layer is about *adjusting proportions*, not adding costs. Users never feel like they're being asked to pay more. The baseline is the baseline. The discretionary layer lets them steer where the surplus goes.   
This three-layer model has a beneficial psychological property: **the default behavior of just watching stuff and not touching any settings produces a fair, complete outcome.** The CRF funds itself. Infrastructure pays for itself. Creators receive what they're owed. A user who never opens a settings page is still a fully functioning participant in the ecosystem. The discretionary layer is for people who care enough to express preference, and it generates a signal that does not exist on any current platform — a value signal distinct from an attention signal.

# How It Works: The Creator Side   
 --- 
## No Tiers, No Invoices, No Sticker Shock   
In V1, creators chose a tier (Seed, Sprout, Bloom, Canopy, Redwood) and received a monthly hosting bill. This created a psychological barrier: a creator researching Bluebell would see "$69/month" or "$1,200/month" and immediately compare it to YouTube ($0) or Patreon ($0 for hosting). The math works in the creator's favor, but you have to get past the sticker price to see it.   
In V2, **creators never receive an infrastructure invoice.** Instead, infrastructure costs are deducted transparently from their pool earnings before payout. The creator sees what they earned and what infrastructure cost, but they never owe anything — the deduction happens at the source.   
Infrastructure costs have two components:   
**Storage cost.** A per-GB/month charge for the creator's video library. Standard storage: ~$0.02/GB/mo. Cold storage (for low-traffic back catalog): ~$0.007/GB/mo. This cost exists whether anyone watches or not, but it's small — a 77-video channel like Life Of Riza has ~69 GB of storage, costing ~$1.38/mo.   
**Delivery cost.** A per-view-minute charge for bandwidth consumed when viewers watch the creator's content. This scales perfectly: zero views = zero delivery cost. More views = higher delivery cost, but also proportionally higher earnings. At volume, the delivery cost is roughly $0.00002–0.00004 per view-minute — roughly 0.2–0.4% of the pool payout rate for that same view-minute.   
   
## What the Creator Sees   
The creator's earnings dashboard could present this as:   
```
This month's earnings:
- Total view time: 5.28M minutes
- Pool earnings: $1,575.00
- Infrastructure: −$69.00
	- Storage (69 GB): −$1.38
	- Delivery (21 TB): −$67.62
- Net earnings: $1,506.00
	- You kept 95.6% of gross revenue.
```
Compare this to YouTube, where the equivalent creator sees:   
```
- YouTube ad revenue: $1,575.00
- YouTube's cut (45%): −$708.75
- Net earnings: $866.25
- You kept 55% of gross revenue.

```
Same top-line. Bluebell creator keeps $1,506. YouTube creator keeps $866. And the Bluebell creator never saw a tier page, never chose a plan, never received an invoice.   
   
## The CRF Safety Net   
For creators whose pool earnings are less than their infrastructure costs — typically very small or very new creators — the Community Resilience Fund covers the gap. The creator still sees the breakdown in their dashboard, but the net line never goes negative:   
```
This month's earnings
- Total view time: 155K minutes
- Pool earnings: $11.00
- Infrastructure: −$29.00
	- Storage (810 GB): −$16.20
	- Delivery (620 GB): −$12.80
- Community Resilience Fund: +$18.00
- Net earnings: $11.00

The community covered $18 of your infrastructure costs this month. We're rooting for you!
As your audience grows, your viewer-funded delivery will cover more of this automatically.
```
This is the "free until you're profitable" dynamic. The creator never pays out of pocket. The subsidy tapers automatically as viewership grows and delivery costs become viewer-funded. The dashboard makes this visible and motivating — the creator can watch the CRF subsidy shrink month over month as their audience grows.

# Self-Hosting and CDN-Assisted Local Storage   
 --- 
## The Self-Hosting Escape Valve   
A foundational design principle: **managed hosting is a convenience, never a requirement.** Any creator can run their own Bluebell node on their own infrastructure at any time. The node software is open source. A technically inclined creator can set up their own server, install the node software, and operate independently.   
This keeps the managed hosting service honest. If pricing becomes extractive, creators leave. Unlike YouTube — where "leaving" means abandoning your channel, subscriber base, and entire history — a Bluebell creator can switch hosting providers while keeping their identity, audience relationships, and content on the network. The hosting service competes on convenience and reliability, not on lock-in.   
## CDN-Assisted Local Storage   
The creator comparison table reveals that some creators have infrastructure profiles where storage dominates cost, not bandwidth. LittleDuck's 1,100-video library needs ~2.9 TB of storage but only generates 1.76 TB/mo of bandwidth. Amaiguri's 270 videos need ~810 GB but generate only 620 GB/mo of bandwidth. For these creators, paying for cloud storage of files that rarely get watched is the majority of their cost.   
CDN-assisted local storage solves this by splitting the concern: **the creator stores their video files locally (on their own hardware), and Bluebell provides CDN-only service** — an origin-pull cache fronting the creator's local machine so viewers are served from edge nodes, not from the creator's upload pipe.   
### How it works   
1. Creator runs a lightweight app on their home machine (or any machine they control). The app manages their video library, handles local transcoding, and exposes an origin endpoint via an automatic tunnel (Cloudflare Tunnel or similar — no port forwarding, no router configuration required).   
2. Bluebell's CDN is configured as an origin-pull cache for that creator's node. When a viewer requests a video, the CDN checks its edge cache first. On a cache hit (common for recent/popular videos), the viewer is served directly from the edge — the creator's home connection is never touched. On a cache miss (rare, typically for old back-catalog content), the CDN pulls from the creator's local machine, caches it, and serves subsequent requests from the edge.   
3. The creator pays only for CDN delivery bandwidth, not for cloud storage. Their local hard drive is the storage layer. The CDN cost is deducted from pool earnings like any other infrastructure cost, but it's dramatically lower because there's no $0.02/GB/mo storage charge.   
   
### Who benefits most   
CDN-assisted local storage is ideal for creators with **large libraries and moderate viewership** — the "deep catalog" profile:   
|                                Creator Profile |                      Cloud Storage Cost | CDN-Only Delivery Cost |                 Savings |
|:-----------------------------------------------|:----------------------------------------|:-----------------------|:------------------------|
| LittleDuck (1.1K videos, 2.9 TB, 20K views/mo) | $58/mo storage + $11/mo delivery = ~$69 |  ~$11/mo delivery only | ~$58/mo (84% reduction) |
|   Amaiguri (270 videos, 810 GB, 6.2K views/mo) |  $11/mo storage + $4/mo delivery = ~$15 |   ~$4/mo delivery only | ~$11/mo (73% reduction) |

For bandwidth-heavy creators (MAPHRA, Hank Green), local storage saves less because delivery is the dominant cost regardless. But for the deep-catalog creators who are most likely to be CRF-subsidized, local storage can be the difference between needing community support and being self-sustaining.   
### The local app   
The CDN-assisted model requires a local application that creators run on their machine. This should be genuinely easy to use — not a developer tool, but a consumer product:   
- **Zero-config networking.** Automatic Cloudflare Tunnel on startup. No port forwarding, no dynamic DNS, no router configuration. Plug in ethernet (or connect to WiFi), the app registers itself with Bluebell's CDN.   
- **Local web dashboard.** A clean UI accessible from any device on the local network showing the video library, storage usage, which videos are being served, bandwidth stats, and CDN cache status.   
- **Drag-and-drop uploads.** Drop a video file into the web UI (or a watched folder on disk), and the app transcodes to adaptive bitrate tiers locally. Transcoding is slower on consumer hardware than cloud — a 10-minute 1080p video might take 20–30 minutes on a typical desktop CPU — but the creator doesn't need it instantly because the CDN will warm the cache once it's ready.   
- **Smart cache coordination.** The app tells Bluebell's CDN what content is available and at what quality tiers. The CDN pulls on demand and caches aggressively. Popular recent content stays warm at the edge indefinitely. Archival content is pulled on the rare cache miss.   
- **Graceful degradation.** If the creator's machine goes offline (power outage, internet hiccup, reboot), the CDN continues serving from its cache for as long as cached content remains valid. Popular videos may be available for hours or days from the edge with no origin. The dashboard shows the creator their uptime and any cache misses that occurred during downtime.   
- **Automatic cold/warm management.** Frequently-requested videos on fast storage, rarely-watched back catalog can overflow to an attached USB drive or network storage.   
   
### Practical limits of home hosting   
CDN-assisted local storage works well within certain bounds:   
- **Upload bandwidth.** The creator's home connection only needs to handle CDN origin pulls (cache misses), not direct viewer traffic. For a creator with 20K monthly views and good CDN cache hit rates (90%+), that's ~200 cache misses per day — trivial even on a modest connection. The ceiling becomes real for creators experiencing rapid growth or premiere-style events where many videos are accessed simultaneously before caching.   
- **Reliability.** Content availability depends on the home machine being online. For hobbyist creators, occasional downtime is fine — the CDN cache covers short gaps. For creators whose content is a primary income source, the managed hosting service (with SLA guarantees) may be a better fit.   
- **ISP terms of service.** Some residential ISPs prohibit running servers, though enforcement for low-traffic origin servers behind a CDN tunnel is rare. The tunnel-based approach (outbound connection only, no inbound ports) avoids most ToS concerns.   
   
   
## The Hosting Box   
For creators who want CDN-assisted local storage but don't want to configure a desktop app or dedicate their personal computer to the task, Bluebell could offer a **Hosting Box**: a small, purpose-built appliance that plugs into the creator's router and acts as their personal node.   
### Hardware concept   
A minimal appliance built around commodity components:   
|     Component |                                                     Spec |            Cost |
|:--------------|:---------------------------------------------------------|:----------------|
|       Compute |                   ARM SBC (Raspberry Pi 5 or equivalent) |         ~$60–80 |
|       Storage |                                     4 TB 2.5" HDD or SSD |        ~$80–100 |
|     Enclosure | Custom case with passive cooling, power supply, ethernet |         ~$20–30 |
|      Software |            Pre-installed Bluebell node + local dashboard |   $0 (marginal) |
| **Total BOM** |                                                          |   **~$160–210** |

Sell at $249–299, or near-cost as a growth investment. Every box sold is a creator who's now self-sustaining on storage and paying the CDN delivery fee (deducted from earnings) indefinitely. The recurring CDN revenue over the life of the device more than justifies a subsidized hardware price.   
### Setup experience   
1. Plug the box into your router via ethernet.   
2. Plug in power.   
3. Open the Bluebell app on your phone or computer. The box appears automatically (mDNS/Bonjour discovery).   
4. Log in with your Bluebell identity. The box registers as your node.   
5. Upload videos through the local web dashboard, or transfer existing content from a USB drive.   
6. The box handles transcoding, serves as CDN origin, and manages your library. You never think about it again.   
   
The box should be a "set it and forget it" appliance. No SSH, no Docker, no configuration files. Updates happen automatically. The creator interacts with it through a web UI that looks like any other media management app.   
### Build-your-own option   
For technically inclined creators who'd rather build their own equivalent, Bluebell should publish complete documentation:   
- **Recommended hardware specs.** Minimum: any ARM or x86 machine with 2+ GB RAM and a hard drive. A Raspberry Pi 4/5, an old laptop, a NAS, or a mini PC all work. The node software is lightweight.   
- **Installation guide.** One-command install script for common Linux distributions (Ubuntu, Debian, Raspberry Pi OS). Docker image available for more complex setups.   
- **Network configuration.** The software handles tunnel setup automatically, but documentation should cover manual Cloudflare Tunnel configuration, dynamic DNS alternatives, and the (rare) cases where a creator might want to expose ports directly.   
- **Storage expansion.** How to add external drives, configure RAID for redundancy (if desired), and set up tiered storage (fast SSD for recent content, large HDD for archive).   
   
The open documentation reinforces the same principle as open-source node software: the Hosting Box is a convenience product, not a requirement. Anyone can replicate it with off-the-shelf hardware and free software. Bluebell sells the box for people who don't want to — and earns their trust by being transparent about exactly what's inside.   
   
# Cost Reduction Strategies   
 --- 
These optimizations reduce raw infrastructure costs across the entire platform, benefiting both managed hosting customers and the sustainability of the CRF.   
## Adaptive quality tier management   
Not every video needs every quality tier stored permanently. The node software (both managed and self-hosted) can automatically manage storage: keep the latest N videos at full quality tiers (360p through 1080p), and for older back-catalog videos with low view counts, store only 720p + 1080p. If a viewer requests 480p for an old video, transcode on-the-fly or serve 720p. This reduces storage costs by 30–40% for creators with large libraries.   
## Intelligent caching   
Most creators follow a power-law view distribution — recent videos get most of the traffic, old videos get occasional views. The CDN edge cache naturally handles this, but the node software can optimize further by pre-warming popular content and lazy-loading archival content from cold storage. DigitalOcean Spaces Cold Storage at $0.007/GiB/month (vs. $0.02/GiB standard) significantly reduces the cost of large back catalogs.   
## Peer-assisted delivery   
For videos with many concurrent viewers (premiere events, viral moments), WebRTC-based peer-assisted delivery can dramatically reduce CDN bandwidth. If 1,000 people are watching the same video simultaneously, each viewer can serve chunks to nearby peers, reducing origin bandwidth by 40–80%. This is specifically useful during surge events the CRF covers, directly reducing the fund's payout obligations.   
## Shared infrastructure (managed hosting)   
Managed hosting runs many creator nodes on shared infrastructure. A single high-spec Droplet can host 10–20 small creator nodes via containerization (Docker/Kubernetes). The per-creator cost of compute drops dramatically with multi-tenancy, even though each creator's content and data remain isolated. This is how very small creators can exist on managed infrastructure at negligible marginal cost.   
## Volume pricing   
At scale, the managed hosting service negotiates volume pricing with infrastructure providers. DigitalOcean offers custom pricing for high-volume customers. The retail prices used in this document are worst-case; actual costs at platform scale would be meaningfully lower.   
   
# How This All Connects   
 --- 
The V2 model simplifies the system into a clean flow:   
1. **Subscribers** pay $10/month.   
2. **3% goes to the Community Resilience Fund** — covering small creator subsidies and viral surge protection.   
3. The remainder flows through **Infrastructure Baseline** (automatic, per-view-minute deduction covering storage + delivery costs) and then **Creator Allocation** (Baseline proportional to watch time + Discretionary manual adjustments).   
4. Creators receive pool income with infrastructure already deducted — **no invoice, no tier selection, no sticker shock.**   
5. Creators who want to reduce their infrastructure footprint can **self-host with CDN assistance**, storing files locally and paying only for delivery.   
6. The managed hosting margin and CDN fees fund **platform development and shared infrastructure.**   
   
The net effect: a creator's financial experience of the platform is always positive. At every stage of growth, what they receive from the subscription pool exceeds what they pay for infrastructure — either because the CRF covers the gap (early stage) or because infrastructure costs are a tiny fraction of earnings (growth stage and beyond). No creator is ever punished for being small. No creator is ever punished for going viral. No creator ever sees a bill that makes them question whether they can afford to be here.   
   
# Open Questions   
 --- 
1. **Infrastructure baseline precision.** The per-view-minute infrastructure cost varies by creator (different storage profiles, different CDN configurations). Should each creator have a precisely computed infrastructure rate, or should the platform use a blended average across all creators? Precise computation is fairer but more complex. A blended rate is simpler but effectively cross-subsidizes high-bandwidth creators.   
2. **CRF governance.** Who decides the fund allocation percentage? Who audits payouts? A foundation board, a creator council, an algorithmic formula, or some combination? Transparency is essential — subscribers should be able to see exactly how much goes to the fund and what it covers.   
3. **Self-hosting incentive balance.** CDN-assisted local storage saves the platform money (no storage costs) and saves the creator money (lower infrastructure deduction). But it also reduces managed hosting revenue. Is this a tension, or does the overall ecosystem health from more self-sustaining creators outweigh the revenue impact?   
4. **Hosting Box economics.** At what scale does manufacturing a custom appliance become viable? Early on, a Raspberry Pi in a nice case with a pre-flashed SD card might be sufficient. At scale, a custom PCB with integrated storage could reduce BOM cost and improve reliability. The transition point matters for pricing strategy.   
5. **Geographic cost variation.** DigitalOcean pricing is flat across regions, which is unusual and helpful. But if creators serve audiences in regions where bandwidth is expensive (parts of Asia, South America), CDN costs may vary. Region-aware infrastructure pricing or audience-weighted delivery optimization may be needed.   
6. **4K and future codecs.** 4K roughly quadruples storage and bandwidth costs versus 1080p. Next-gen codecs (AV1, ~30% better compression than H.264) could bring 4K costs closer to current 1080p levels. The infrastructure baseline rate should anticipate codec improvements over time.   
7. **The deep-catalog cold storage transition.** For managed hosting, at what point should old videos automatically move to cold storage ($0.007/GB vs. $0.02/GB)? What's the retrieval latency penalty, and is it acceptable for occasional views of archival content? This directly affects creators like LittleDuck whose storage costs dominate.
